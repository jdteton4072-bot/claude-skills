## Bottom Line (read this first)

1. **The most launch-threatening constraint is not gaming licensing — it is distribution and payments.** Google Ads, Meta, and PayPal each have written policies that expressly capture "tips/picks" services, and Stripe's restricted-business list expressly names "sports forecasting or odds making." A picks subscription is a **policy-prohibited or permission-gated business on the four largest acquisition and payment rails simultaneously**. This is not a legal risk you can lawyer around; it is a platform-contract risk that can zero the business overnight with no due process. **Confidence: High** on the existence of the policies; **Medium** on how each platform applies them to a paid picks subscription that carries no sportsbook affiliate links. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
2. **A quantitative performance claim like "58% ATS" is the single highest-probability enforcement trigger,** more likely than any gaming statute. FTC law requires a *reasonable basis in hand before dissemination* (1984 Advertising Substantiation Policy Statement; *Pfizer*, 81 F.T.C. 23 (1972)), and the only U.S. criminal case squarely on a sports tout — *United States v. Adam Meyer* / Real Money Sports (E.D. Wis., indicted 2015, 8-year sentence 2017) — was built on **inflated win-percentage representations**, prosecuted as wire fraud, not as a gambling offense. **Confidence: High** that inflated/unsupported win-rate claims are the primary legal exposure. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
3. **"For entertainment purposes only" is largely folk wisdom.** No authority located holding that such a disclaimer converts a deceptive performance claim into a lawful one. FTC's Deception Policy Statement (Oct. 14, 1983) and its enforcement position are that a disclaimer cannot cure a claim that is deceptive in its main message. It may have *some* value on the separate question of whether you are "promoting gambling" under a state penal statute, but **no case located** so holding. **Confidence: High** that it does not cure deception; **Low** on any protective value against state gambling-promotion statutes. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
4. **The brief's premise about Nevada appears to be wrong, and the correction cuts in your favor.** Nevada Gaming Commission **Regulation 5A is "Operation of Interactive Gaming"** (adopted Dec. 22, 2011), not an information-service regime. Nevada's "information service" license (NRS 463.01642 / NRS 463.160) is defined as a person who **sells information *to a licensed sports pool***, i.e., B2B to a sportsbook — not a consumer-facing tout. **I located no state in the U.S. with an express licensing or registration regime for consumer-facing sports-pick sellers.** **Confidence: Medium-High** on the Nevada statutory text; **Medium** on the negative finding for other states (a negative finding from search is inherently weaker than a positive one). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
5. **Two real state criminal statutes do reach the *transmission of gambling information* and are not cured by the product not taking bets:** Oklahoma 21 O.S. § 987 (dissemination of gambling information — felony) and Illinois 720 ILCS 5/28-1(a)(9) (knowingly transmitting information as to wagers or betting odds). Both contain narrow exceptions for licensed broadcasters / news reporting that a paid subscription picks service likely does not fit. **Confidence: High** that the statutes exist and are facially broad; **Medium-Low** on whether a modern prosecutor would apply them to a subscription analytics product — **no enforcement action against a picks subscription located under either statute.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

## 1. Federal Layer

### 1.1 Wire Act — 18 U.S.C. § 1084

**The gating element is "engaged in the business of betting or wagering," not the content transmitted.** Section 1084(a) reaches "whoever being engaged in the business of betting or wagering knowingly uses a wire communication facility for the transmission in interstate or foreign commerce of bets or wagers or **information assisting in the placing of bets or wagers** on any sporting event or contest."

- The predicate has been construed to require that the defendant "regularly devoted time, attention and labor to betting or wagering **for profit**" — a bookmaking/gambling-business posture, not a bettor and not, on its face, a seller of opinions. The Wire Act "apparently cannot be used to prosecute simple bettors" (Congressional Research Service, *Internet Gambling: An Overview of Federal Criminal Law*, RL97-619). **Confidence: Medium-High** that a subscription picks service that never accepts, records, forwards, or profits from wagers falls outside § 1084(a). **This is an interpretive conclusion, not a holding — I located no case applying § 1084 to a pure paid tout service.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- *United States v. Lyons*, 740 F.3d 702 (1st Cir. 2014) rejected the argument that the Act reaches only entities devoted *exclusively* to sports betting — i.e., courts read the "business" element generously once a gambling business exists. Relevance here: **do not take affiliate commissions tied to wagering volume, do not run any pool, contest with entry fees, or "bet with us" feature**, because revenue tied to wagering activity is the fact pattern that most plausibly drags an information seller across the "engaged in the business" line. **Confidence: Medium** (extrapolation from the element, not from an on-point holding). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **§ 1084(b) safe harbor:** transmission of "information for use in news reporting of sporting events," and transmission of "information assisting in the placing of bets or wagers... from a State... where betting on that sporting event or contest is legal into a State... in which such betting is legal." **Confidence: High** on the text. Two cautions: (i) the safe harbor only matters if you are otherwise "engaged in the business," so it is a backstop, not the primary defense; (ii) it is expressly **bilateral** — legal-origin *and* legal-destination. On its own terms it gives no cover for transmitting picks into Utah, Hawaii, Texas, California, Georgia, Alabama, Minnesota, Idaho, Oklahoma, South Carolina, or Alaska. **This is the single strongest textual argument for geo-blocking.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**DOJ opinions and litigation.**
- OLC opinion of Sept. 20, 2011 (released Dec. 23, 2011): Wire Act prohibitions apply only to sports wagering.
- OLC opinion of Nov. 2, 2018 (released Jan. 14, 2019): reversed, applying the Act to non-sports gambling.
- *New Hampshire Lottery Comm'n v. Rosen (Barr)*, 986 F.3d 1 (1st Cir. Jan. 20, 2021): affirmed that § 1084 is limited to bets or wagers on **sporting events or contests**.
- **Net effect for this business: none of this narrows your exposure, because your subject matter is sports.** The 2011/2018/2021 fight was about whether *non-sports* gambling is covered. Your product sits inside the part of the statute that everyone agrees is covered *as to subject matter*; your defense rests entirely on the "engaged in the business of betting or wagering" element. **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **No authority located** for a formal DOJ rescission of the 2018 opinion; the First Circuit judgment binds within that circuit and DOJ did not seek certiorari. **Confidence: Medium.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 1.2 UIGEA — 31 U.S.C. §§ 5361–5367

- UIGEA § 5363 prohibits a **person engaged in the business of betting or wagering** from *knowingly accepting* payment (credit, EFT, checks, etc.) "in connection with the participation of another person in unlawful Internet gambling."
- § 5362(1)(A) defines "bet or wager" as **staking or risking something of value** on an outcome. A subscription fee for information is not a stake on a contest outcome — the subscriber gets the picks regardless of the game result. **Confidence: High** that a flat subscription fee is not a "bet or wager" under § 5362(1)(A). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- § 5362(2) excludes from "business of betting or wagering" the activities of financial transaction providers, interactive computer services, and telecommunications services; § 5367 preserves liability where such an entity is directly engaged in operating an internet gambling site. **A pure information seller is outside UIGEA's operative prohibition. Confidence: Medium-High** — this is a reading of the definitions, not a holding on a tout service. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Operational sting in the tail:** UIGEA drove the banking system's coding and blocking practices (Regulation GG). Banks and acquirers de-risk by *category*, not by legal analysis. This is the mechanism by which a legally-outside-UIGEA business still gets classified into MCC 7995 and dropped. See § 3.5. **Confidence: High** on the practical dynamic; **Medium** on the specific causal chain.
- **Contingent pricing warning:** if pricing is ever made outcome-contingent — "pay only if we go 55%+," "money-back if the pick loses," profit-share, or a pick sold at odds — the "staking something of value on an outcome" analysis changes materially and both UIGEA and state gambling definitions come back into play. **Confidence: Medium.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 1.3 PASPA and *Murphy v. NCAA*

*Murphy v. NCAA*, 138 S. Ct. 1461 (May 14, 2018) struck PASPA on anti-commandeering grounds.

- **What it changed for you: essentially nothing directly.** PASPA prohibited *states* from authorizing sports wagering schemes; it never regulated information sellers. Its repeal did not create any federal permission to sell picks.
- **What it did change indirectly:** it created 38+ state regimes, each with its own advertising, affiliate, and responsible-gambling rules that reach *marketing conduct*, and it left every non-legalizing state free to keep its criminal gambling code fully in force. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Do not read Murphy as "sports betting is legal now."** It expressly left states free to prohibit. **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 1.4 FTC — Section 5, substantiation, endorsements

This is the operative federal regime for this business.

**Section 5 / deception.** FTC Act § 5 (15 U.S.C. § 45) prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices. FTC Policy Statement on Deception (Oct. 14, 1983): a representation, omission, or practice that is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably, and is material.

**Substantiation standard for a "58% ATS" claim.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- *Pfizer, Inc.*, 81 F.T.C. 23 (1972) and the **FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation (1984)** establish that an advertiser must possess and rely upon a **reasonable basis** for objective claims **before** dissemination. Failure to do so is itself a § 5 violation, independent of whether the claim later proves true. **Confidence: High** on the standard. **This is the most important sentence in this memo for product design: publishing the number before the evidence file exists is the violation.**
- Where an ad conveys a **specific level of support** ("our model is proven to hit 58%," "backtested over 10 seasons"), the advertiser must possess **at least that level** of support. **Confidence: High.**
- The **"competent and reliable scientific evidence"** formulation — "tests, analyses, research, or studies... conducted and evaluated in an objective manner by persons qualified to do so, using procedures generally accepted in the profession to yield accurate and reliable results" — is the FTC's standard for health/safety/efficacy claims and is routinely imposed in FTC orders. **I located no FTC guidance applying this specific phrase to sports-prediction performance claims. Confidence: Medium** that a court or the FTC would demand a functionally equivalent standard (documented, pre-registered, methodologically defensible record) for a quantitative win-rate claim. This is an extrapolation and I am labeling it as such. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Documentation to retain (research-derived best practice, not a located FTC record-retention rule for this claim type — no authority located specifying a retention period for general advertising substantiation):**
  - Immutable, timestamped record of every pick at time of publication (pick, line, book, timestamp) — the line you were graded against must be captured *before* the game, not reconstructed.
  - The exact grading methodology: push handling, cancelled/postponed games, half-point differences, closing vs. taken line, unit weighting.
  - Complete-record accounting — no dropped periods, no "since we improved the model" cherry-picking. Selective-period claims are the classic deception vector.
  - The claim substantiation file itself: the specific sentence in the ad, the dataset, the computation, the date, and who approved it.
  - **Live forward-tested results should be segregated from backtested results, and the ad must say which it is.** Presenting a backtest as a track record is the sharpest deception risk in this product category. **Confidence: Medium** (reasoned from the substantiation standard; no on-point FTC action on sports models located). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**Endorsement Guides — 16 C.F.R. Part 255 (revised final Guides, 2023).** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- Advertisers are liable for claims **conveyed by** an endorsement and must have substantiation for them (§ 255.1). A customer saying "I made $40,000" is an efficacy claim you must substantiate as if you had said it.
- Material connections (free subscriptions, commissions, discounts, affiliate payouts) must be **clearly and conspicuously** disclosed (§ 255.5); the 2023 revision tightened "clear and conspicuous" (unavoidable, same medium, no burying in a bio or "#ad" in a hashtag block).
- Atypical results require disclosure of generally expected performance; a "typical results may vary" disclaimer does not cure a misleading atypical testimonial. **Confidence: High.**
- **Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule, 16 C.F.R. Part 465** (final rule published Aug. 2024, effective Oct. 21, 2024): bans fake/AI-generated reviews, insider reviews without disclosure, and **conditional incentivized reviews** (compensation contingent on positive sentiment). Carries **civil penalties** (statutory maximum, inflation-adjusted, ~$51,744 per violation). This is a materially harsher posture than the Guides, which are not independently enforceable. **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**FTC enforcement touching gambling-adjacent performance claims.** I located **no FTC enforcement action, warning letter, or business-guidance document specifically directed at sports handicapping or tout services.** **This is a genuine "no authority located."** The nearest analogues are (i) DOJ wire-fraud prosecution of a tout on win-rate misrepresentations (*Meyer*, above), and (ii) heavy state-regulator enforcement against sportsbook "risk-free"/"free bet" claims (Ohio fines of $150,000–$350,000 against three major sportsbooks). Do not read the absence of FTC tout cases as safety — read it as an untested area. **Confidence: High** that no such FTC action was located; **Low** on what it implies. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 1.5 CFTC / sports event contracts — brief note

Kalshi/Robinhood-style sports event contracts are in an active circuit split as of this research date: *KalshiEx LLC v. Flaherty*, No. 25-1922 (3d Cir. Apr. 6, 2026) (2-1; sports event contracts are "swaps," state gambling law preempted, NJ enjoined); Ninth Circuit (Aug. 2026) declined to find preemption of Nevada's authority. Supreme Court resolution appears likely.

**Why it matters to you, in one paragraph:** if event contracts are ultimately held to be CFTC-exclusive, they become legally available in states where sportsbooks are not — which would make picks *usable* in Texas, California, and Georgia and would undercut the geo-blocking rationale. Conversely, Ohio's regulator has already pushed sportsbook licensees to make **affiliates stop promoting Kalshi/Robinhood/Crypto.com** (Mar. 31 cease-and-desist posture reported 2026). **Practical rule: do not market your picks as being "for" prediction markets, and do not take prediction-market affiliate revenue, until the split resolves.** **Confidence: Medium** (fast-moving, genuinely unsettled). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]` **Not researched further per scope.**

## 2. State Layer

### 2.1 Is there a "tout license" anywhere? — Nevada, corrected

**Correction to the brief's premise.** Nevada Gaming Commission **Regulation 5A is "Operation of Interactive Gaming,"** adopted effective **Dec. 22, 2011** under A.B. 258 (2011 session). It is an online-gaming operator/service-provider regime. **It is not an information-service or tout regime.** **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

Nevada's actual information-service provisions:
- **NRS 463.01642 — "Information service" defined:** "a person who sells and provides information **to a licensed sports pool** that is used primarily to aid the placing of wagers on events of any kind," including information/advice/consultation "considered by a licensee in establishing or setting any line, point spread or odds," and "advice, estimate or prediction regarding the outcome of an event." **Excludes** newspapers, magazines of general circulation, and TV/radio services whose primary purpose is other than aiding the placing of wagers.
- **NRS 463.160(1)** makes it unlawful to "provide or maintain any information service" without procuring the required licenses; willful violation of NRS 463.160(1) carries **1–20 years' imprisonment and/or a fine up to $50,000**.

**Reading:** the defined term is **B2B to a licensed Nevada sports pool**. A consumer-facing subscription sold to bettors is not selling "to a licensed sports pool." On the statutory text, **Nevada's information-service license does not appear to reach a B2C picks subscription.** **Confidence: Medium-High** on the definitional text (two independent sources returned the same language); **Medium** on the conclusion, because NRS 463.160's prohibitory language and the definition are in tension and I could not retrieve the full operative subsection text (the Nevada Legislature, Justia, and gaming.nv.gov are blocked from this research environment — **this is a research limitation you should have counsel close**). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**Fees:** **no authority located** for a Nevada information-service license fee schedule specific to information services. Reported adjacent figures — $500 annual (NRS 463.660 context) and $1,000 annual for gaming service providers (NRS 463.677 / Reg. 5A.220) — are **not confirmed as applying to information services**. Treat as unverified. **Confidence: Low.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**Extraterritorial reach over an out-of-state operator serving Nevada residents:** **no authority located.** NRS 463.160 is framed as conduct "in this State." Whether Nevada asserts jurisdiction over a remote website with Nevada subscribers is unresolved in the materials I found. **Confidence: Low — do not assume either direction.** Note as a live datapoint: the Nevada Gaming Control Board held a **2025 workshop on creating a registration category for third-party sports-data/information providers**, which signals the Board views the current framework as *not* capturing these entities and is considering closing that gap. **Confidence: Medium** (trade press; primary Board record not retrievable here). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 2.2 The eleven states checked — NJ, TN, MI, PA, CO, OH, NY, IL, IN, AZ, MA

**Finding: I located no express statutory or regulatory licensing/registration regime for consumer-facing sports-tout, handicapping, or sports-advisory services in any of these eleven states.** Their sports-wagering acts license **operators** and license or register **suppliers/vendors** — i.e., entities that provide goods or services *to a licensed sportsbook*. A B2C picks subscription with no sportsbook contract is outside that hierarchy on its face. **Confidence: Medium.** This is a negative finding derived from search rather than from a completed statute-by-statute review of eleven codes, and negative findings are weak. **Do not treat this as cleared.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**Two important qualifications:**

1. **The moment you take sportsbook affiliate revenue, most of these states capture you.** Affiliate/marketing-affiliate registration or licensing is required in numerous states; reported fee levels include **Tennessee ~$500, Colorado ~$500, Michigan ~$200, New Jersey vendor registration free**, with renewals in most states other than NJ, and requirements often varying by revenue model (CPA vs. revenue share). **Confidence: Medium** (secondary/industry sources; fee levels should be verified against each regulator's current schedule). **Design implication: subscription-only revenue keeps you out of an entire licensing layer. Adding "bet this at DraftKings" affiliate links moves you into it in ~15+ states at once.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

2. **Ohio reaches non-licensee marketers directly.** Ohio Admin. Code **3775-16-08** (advertising) provides that **affiliate marketers need not obtain a supplier license but must comply with all aspects of the advertising rule** and must not advertise forms of illegal gambling in Ohio; sports gaming advertisements may be disseminated in Ohio only for proprietor applicants/licensees unless the ad disclaims Ohio availability. The Ohio Casino Control Commission has actively fined operators over "free/risk-free" promotional language. **Ohio is the state most likely to send you a letter over ad copy. Confidence: Medium-High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 2.3 States where sports betting remains illegal — and the exposure from selling into them

**Reported as still prohibiting sports betting as of 2026:** Alabama, Alaska, California, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Minnesota, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Texas, Utah. (Missouri launched Dec. 1, 2025 and is now legal.) **Confidence: Medium** — this is from aggregated 2026 trade sources, not from eleven primary state codes; verify before configuring geo-blocking. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**The concrete exposure is not "aiding and abetting" in the abstract — it is two specific statute families:**

**(a) Dissemination-of-gambling-information statutes.**
- **Oklahoma, 21 O.S. § 987 — "Dissemination of gambling information":** transmitting or receiving, **by means of any communications facility, information to be used in making or settling bets**; the only carve-out located is for a **licensed radio or television station or newspaper of general circulation** broadcasting odds or results of legally staged sporting events. Penalty: felony, fine up to **$25,000** and/or imprisonment. A paid subscription picks service is not a licensed broadcaster or a newspaper of general circulation. **Confidence: High** that the statute is facially broad enough to reach this conduct; **Medium-Low** on prosecution likelihood — **no enforcement action against a modern picks subscription located.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Illinois, 720 ILCS 5/28-1(a)(9):** knowingly transmitting "information as to wagers, betting odds, or changes in betting odds" by telephone, radio "or similar means," or installing/maintaining equipment for such transmission; exception for **news reporting** of sporting events and for accounting purposes. Class A misdemeanor; Class 4 felony on a second or subsequent conviction under (a)(3)–(a)(12). **Illinois is a legal sports-betting state and still has this on the books.** Article 28 contains exemptions for conduct licensed under Illinois gambling statutes — which a *licensee* enjoys and an unlicensed picks seller does not. **Confidence: High** on the text; **Medium-Low** on application; **the interaction between (a)(9) and the Illinois Sports Wagering Act exemptions is exactly the kind of question that needs a licensed Illinois practitioner, not a researcher.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Other states:** I did not complete a fifty-state survey of dissemination-of-gambling-information statutes. Several additional states are commonly reported to have analogous provisions. **No authority located** for a comprehensive current list. **This is a known gap and it should be closed by counsel before a broad-U.S. launch.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**(b) Gambling-promotion / accessory liability.** General state gambling-promotion statutes (e.g., Texas Penal Code ch. 47, California Penal Code § 337a) are drafted around operating a gambling place, bookmaking, receiving or forwarding bets, or selling chances. **I located no state gambling-promotion statute whose text plainly reaches selling opinions for a flat fee, and no prosecution of a picks seller under one.** **Confidence: Medium** that a pure information sale is outside these statutes; **explicitly labeled uncertain — this is the classic place where a founder reasons by analogy to a comfortable conclusion, and I am declining to.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**(c) First Amendment counterweight — real, but not a shield you can rely on pre-launch.** *Greater New Orleans Broadcasting Ass'n v. United States*, 527 U.S. 173 (1999) unanimously struck 18 U.S.C. § 1304's ban on casino advertising as applied to broadcasters in legal-gambling states, under *Central Hudson*. Truthful, non-misleading commercial speech about lawful activity gets substantial protection. **Two limits:** (i) *Central Hudson* protects only speech that is **not misleading** — an unsubstantiated 58% claim gets no protection at all; (ii) asserting the First Amendment is a **defense you raise after being charged**, which is not a business plan. **Confidence: High** on the case; **Medium** on its practical value here. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**(d) Consumer-protection exposure exists everywhere, including illegal-betting states.** Every state has a UDAP statute; several have private rights of action and statutory damages. A resident of Texas who bought your subscription based on a win-rate claim can sue under the Texas DTPA regardless of whether sports betting is legal there. **Confidence: High** on the general principle. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 2.4 State gambling-advertising rules

These rules are written to bind **licensed operators and, in some states, their affiliates**. As an unaffiliated subscription seller you are likely **outside** most of them by their terms — but they define the market's norms, they define what platforms and processors will tolerate, and Ohio's rule reaches non-licensee affiliate marketers expressly.

- **Ohio:** Ohio Admin. Code 3775-16-08 (above). "Risk-free"/"free bet" language restricted since Jan. 2023; fines $150,000–$350,000 imposed on operators. **Confidence: Medium-High.**
- **Massachusetts:** 205 CMR 256.00 — advertising may not label a promotion "free" if the user must risk their own money; must carry a problem-gambling helpline message; may not target minors or self-excluded persons; **may not suggest that success is guaranteed.** **Confidence: Medium-High.** The "no suggestion that success is guaranteed" norm is the single most directly transferable rule to a picks product.
- **New York:** legislation signed Oct. 1, 2024 requires warning messages about the harmful/addictive effects of gambling in gambling advertisements, including online sports wagering; NYSGC requires problem-gambling messaging on operator advertisements. **Confidence: Medium.**
- **Pennsylvania:** PGCB has restricted "free bet" promotional marketing. **Confidence: Medium** (secondary source).
- **Colorado, Illinois, Maryland, Tennessee:** comparable responsible-gambling messaging and promotional-language rules; Illinois Gaming Board adopted new advertising rules (reported 2025). **Confidence: Low-Medium** — verify per state.
- **American Gaming Association Responsible Marketing Code** (revised to ban "risk-free"): **industry self-regulation, not law**, but it is what platforms and payment partners benchmark against. **Confidence: High** that it is voluntary.
- **Age targeting:** ads must not target persons under the legal wagering age (21 in most states). **Confidence: High** as a general norm.

**Practical translation regardless of technical applicability: never publish "guaranteed," "lock," "risk-free," "can't lose," "free money," or any equivalent. That vocabulary is the single fastest route to a regulator letter, a platform ban, and a UDAP claim.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 2.5 Recommended launch-state posture — three options, ranked

**Option A (RECOMMENDED): Narrow allowlist of clearly permissive states.**
Sell only into states that (i) have legal, regulated online sports wagering, and (ii) have no located dissemination-of-gambling-information statute. On the research above that excludes Illinois from the initial list (720 ILCS 5/28-1(a)(9)) and excludes all eleven prohibition states. A defensible starting allowlist for counsel to validate: **NJ, PA, MI, CO, TN, AZ, IN, OH, VA, IA, WV, KS, LA, MD, MA, NC, KY, MO** — with **NY** and **IL** deferred pending counsel review.
- *Pro:* the § 1084(b) bilateral safe harbor is satisfied on its own terms; you never transmit picks into a state where the underlying activity is criminal; you have a clean story for Stripe/Meta/Apple review; you cap the fifty-state survey cost.
- *Con:* forfeits California and Texas, the two largest consumer markets, and Georgia — plausibly 20–25% of addressable U.S. demand.
- *Tradeoff verdict:* the forfeited demand is the price of a defensible posture. For a solo/small-team builder with no litigation budget, this is the correct trade.

**Option B: Broad U.S. with geo-blocking of problem states.**
Ship everywhere except a blocklist (the eleven prohibition states plus OK and IL).
- *Pro:* materially larger TAM; operationally similar effort to Option A (you are building the geo-gate either way).
- *Con:* an allowlist fails **closed** and a blocklist fails **open**. Any state you failed to survey — and there are states with dissemination statutes I did not enumerate (§ 2.3(a)) — becomes live exposure by default. It also requires you to be right about all fifty codes rather than about eighteen.
- *Tradeoff verdict:* acceptable **only after** counsel completes a fifty-state dissemination-of-gambling-information and gambling-promotion survey. Not acceptable as a launch posture.

**Option C: Broad U.S., no geo-gating, disclaimer-only.**
- **Do not do this.** It is the industry default and it rests on the "entertainment purposes only" belief that § 3.2 shows is unsupported. It forfeits the § 1084(b) argument, maximizes UDAP exposure across fifty AG offices, and gives your payment processor a clean reason to terminate.

**Cross-cutting recommendation for all options:** geo-gating must be **enforced at signup and at content delivery** (IP + billing address + attestation), not merely stated in the terms. A terms-of-service statement that Oklahoma residents "may not subscribe," with no technical enforcement and Oklahoma subscribers on the books, is worse than useless — it establishes that you knew. **Confidence: Medium** (reasoned from general enforcement practice; **no authority located** on the sufficiency of any particular geo-gating standard for this product type). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

## 3. Advertising and Claims Substantiation

### 3.1 What you must have in hand *before* publishing a win-rate claim

Per the 1984 FTC Advertising Substantiation Policy Statement and *Pfizer* (§ 1.4): a **reasonable basis, possessed and relied upon prior to dissemination.** Concretely, before "58% ATS" goes on the landing page:

1. A complete, timestamped, immutable pick log covering the entire claimed period, with the line and book captured at publication time.
2. A written, fixed grading methodology (pushes, cancellations, line selection, unit weighting) applied uniformly — decided *before* results are computed, not after.
3. The exact claim sentence, the dataset it is computed from, the computation, the date computed, and the period covered — as a retained file.
4. Explicit labeling of **backtest vs. live**, with backtested figures never presented as a track record.
5. The claimed period must not be cherry-picked; material adverse periods must not be excluded.
6. A materially conspicuous statement of sample size and the period, adjacent to the number, not in a footer.
7. Re-verification on a schedule; a claim that was true in January and false in November is a live violation in November.

**Confidence: High** on items 1–3 and 7 (direct application of the substantiation standard); **Medium** on 4–6 (reasoned application; no FTC guidance located specific to statistical sports-model claims). `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 3.2 "For entertainment purposes only" — folk wisdom, researched specifically

**Finding: I located no FTC statement, enforcement action, or judicial decision holding that an "entertainment purposes only" disclaimer immunizes a paid picks service.**

- The FTC's position is that **disclaimers do not cure a deceptive main message.** FTC business guidance states plainly that fine-print qualifications are insufficient to remedy a deceptive claim (the FTC's own worked example: "Lose 10 pounds in one week without dieting" is not saved by fine print reading "Diet and exercise required"). Deception is assessed from the **net impression**; disclosures must be **clear and conspicuous**, and even then cannot contradict the principal claim. FTC Policy Statement on Deception (Oct. 14, 1983); FTC "Advertising FAQ's: A Guide for Small Business." **Confidence: High.**
- The 2015 Enforcement Policy Statement on Deceptively Formatted Advertisements reinforces that formatting and placement cannot be used to dilute a material message. **Confidence: High.**
- On the **separate** question — whether the disclaimer helps against a state gambling-promotion or dissemination-of-gambling-information charge — **no authority located either way.** Note that the disclaimer is also in tension with your own marketing: if the site simultaneously says "entertainment only" and "58% ATS, beat the book," a factfinder resolves that against you. **Confidence: Low** on any protective value; **Medium-High** that the internal contradiction is itself a liability. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Where a disclaimer *does* have value:** as a truthful, non-contradicted statement that you do not accept wagers, hold funds, or guarantee outcomes, and that the customer bears all wagering risk. That is a **contract and expectation-setting** function, and it is worth having. It is not a legal shield. **Confidence: Medium.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 3.3 Testimonials, endorsements, affiliates

See § 1.4. Operative rules if you ever use a customer success story or a paid promoter:
- You own and must substantiate every claim the endorsement conveys (16 C.F.R. § 255.1). A screenshot of a subscriber's winning parlay is an efficacy claim about your product.
- Material connections disclosed clearly and conspicuously (§ 255.5) — including free or discounted subscriptions to reviewers.
- Atypical results: disclose generally expected performance; "results not typical" does not cure (§ 255.2).
- 16 C.F.R. Part 465 (eff. Oct. 21, 2024): no fake, AI-generated, or undisclosed insider reviews; **no reviews incentivized on condition of positive sentiment**; civil penalties available.
- **Highest-risk pattern in this category:** an affiliate/influencer posting their own inflated "we went 12-2 last week with [Product]" content. You are exposed for the affiliate's claims. If you run an affiliate program, you need a written policy, pre-approval of claims, and active monitoring. **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

### 3.4 Auto-renewal / negative option — binds the subscription mechanics

**Federal.**
- The FTC's **Negative Option / "Click-to-Cancel" Rule was vacated in its entirety** by the Eighth Circuit on **July 8, 2025** (*Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC*), on the ground that the FTC failed to conduct the required preliminary regulatory analysis. **Confidence: High.**
- The FTC **restarted rulemaking**: draft ANPRM submitted to OIRA **Jan. 30, 2026**; comments due **Apr. 13, 2026**. **As of this research date (Sept. 5, 2026) I located no final replacement rule.** **Confidence: Medium-High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **The vacatur changed almost nothing operationally.** **ROSCA (15 U.S.C. §§ 8401–8405)** remains in force and requires clear and conspicuous disclosure of material terms before obtaining billing information, express informed consent, and a **simple mechanism to stop recurring charges**. FTC enforcement under ROSCA and § 5 has continued through 2026. **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

**State — California is the binding constraint and it is stricter than the vacated federal rule.**
- **California ARL, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 17600 et seq., as amended by AB 2863 (2024), effective July 1, 2025**, applying to contracts entered into, amended, or extended on or after that date. Requirements include: expanded scope covering **free-to-pay conversions/free trials**; **express affirmative consent** to the auto-renewal terms specifically (separate from consent to the purchase); **annual renewal reminders**; clear and conspicuous notice of material price or service changes; **same-medium cancellation** ("click to quit") with a cancellation mechanism no harder than signup; and **retention of consent records for at least three years, or one year after termination, whichever is longer.** **Confidence: High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Other states:** a majority of states now have auto-renewal statutes (New York, Colorado, Virginia, Illinois, Oregon, Minnesota and others), with varying notice and cancellation requirements. **No authority located** for a current comprehensive fifty-state chart in this research. **Confidence: Medium** on the general proliferation; **Low** on any specific non-California state's requirements. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Practical rule: build to California's ARL as the floor.** It is stricter than ROSCA and stricter than the vacated federal rule, it applies to any California subscriber regardless of where you incorporate, and building to it once satisfies most other states. **Retain consent records for 3+ years from day one** — this is a build requirement, not a legal-review item.

**Why this section matters more than it looks:** a picks product has a structurally high refund/chargeback rate (customers who lose money want their money back). Sloppy cancellation mechanics convert unhappy customers into chargebacks, chargebacks convert into acquirer termination (§ 3.5), and a pattern of them attracts both AG and FTC attention. **The auto-renewal design is where the compliance risk and the payment risk are the same risk.**

### 3.5 Platform and payment-processor policy — not law, but binding in practice

**This is the section that most likely determines whether the business can exist.**

**Payment-processor risk in concrete terms.** If an acquirer classifies you under **MCC 7995 (Betting/Casino Gambling/Lottery)**, the consequences are: high-risk classification by **all major card networks**; interchange and processing costs commonly **2x–5x** standard-risk; **lower chargeback thresholds**; **rolling reserves frequently in double-digit percentages** of volume held for months; and a requirement for a specialist high-risk acquirer with gambling expertise plus enhanced KYC/AML. **Confidence: Medium-High** (consistent across multiple payments-industry sources; these are commercial norms, not regulations, and vary by acquirer).

**The compounding failure mode you must plan for:** picks subscriptions generate above-average chargebacks (losing customers dispute). Elevated chargebacks trigger network monitoring programs, which triggers reclassification or termination, which — combined with a rolling reserve — can strand months of revenue. **Mitigations to build in from day one: generous and frictionless self-serve cancellation, a clear refund policy, pre-charge renewal reminders, unambiguous billing descriptors, and a documented dispute-response process.** These are the same controls the ARL/ROSCA section requires. **Confidence: Medium** (operational best practice, not legal requirement).

**Bottom line for § 3.5:** get **written** confirmation of merchant acceptability from your processor, and written ad-account authorization status from Meta/Google, **before** writing production code. If two of the four rails say no, the unit economics change so much that the build plan should be re-scoped. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`

## 4. Consumer-Protection and Duty-of-Care Layer

### 4.1 Age verification — required vs. practiced

- **I located no federal or state statute requiring age verification for the sale of sports *information* to consumers.** The 21-and-over (or 18-and-over) requirements are imposed on **licensed wagering operators**, not on information sellers. **Confidence: Medium** — this is a negative finding. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Underlying age thresholds in wagering states:** 21 in the large majority; **18 in Kentucky, Montana, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, Washington D.C., and Wyoming.** **Confidence: Medium** (secondary sources; verify per state).
- **Industry practice and platform policy effectively impose 21+.** Meta requires gambling ads not target under-18s; state advertising rules bar targeting persons under the legal wagering age. **Recommendation: gate at 21+ with a date-of-birth attestation at signup, with a 21+ age rating on any app listing.** Attestation is not verification; document the choice. **Confidence: Medium.**
- **App-store age-verification statutes are a live and unsettled overlay.** Utah's App Store Accountability Act (S.B. 142, effective May 7, 2025, compliance deadline May 6, 2026) and Texas S.B. 2420 (signed May 27, 2025, effective Jan. 1, 2026) require app stores to verify age category at account creation, obtain parental consent for minors, and **transmit age/consent signals to developers** — creating developer-side obligations to honor those signals. Texas S.B. 2420 was **preliminarily enjoined Dec. 23, 2025** on First Amendment grounds; subsequent Supreme Court activity in 2026 is reported but I could not confirm the outcome from primary sources. Utah's act is also under First Amendment challenge; Louisiana has a comparable act. **Confidence: Medium** on the statutes; **Low** on their current enforceability — **genuinely unsettled, do not build to a fixed assumption.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **COPPA** applies under 13; a 21+ gate plus no child-directed content makes this low-salience. **Confidence: Medium.**

### 4.2 Responsible-gambling obligations

- **Statutory RG mandates — helpline messaging, self-exclusion interaction, problem-gambling disclosures — attach to licensed sports wagering operators (and in some states their affiliates), not to unaffiliated information sellers.** Massachusetts (205 CMR 256.00) requires operator advertising to carry the DPH helpline; New York requires warning messages in gambling advertisements (legislation signed Oct. 1, 2024); Ohio, Colorado, and others impose comparable operator obligations. **Confidence: Medium-High** that these bind operators; **Medium** that they do not reach you absent an affiliate relationship. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Self-exclusion lists: no authority located** requiring a non-licensee information seller to screen against state self-exclusion registries — and in most states those registries are not available to non-licensees. **Confidence: Medium** on the negative. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Helpline numbers are state-specific:** 1-800-GAMBLER (NJ, OH and now widely used), 1-800-522-4700 (CO), 1-800-BETS-OFF (IA), 1-800-9-WITH-IT (IN), 888-BETS-OFF (MO). **Confidence: Medium.**
- **Recommendation notwithstanding the absence of a mandate:** carry a persistent, clear responsible-gambling message and helpline reference, a 21+ notice, and a "wager only what you can afford to lose" statement. Rationale is not legal compliance — it is (i) Meta/Apple/Stripe review posture, (ii) mitigation in any future UDAP or duty-of-care claim, and (iii) the direction of travel in litigation: gambling-harm class actions against operators are an active and growing area as of 2025–2026 (see Venable, *Bet the Company: Are Sports Gambling Class Actions the Next Tobacco?*, Apr. 2025 — **secondary source**). **Confidence: Medium.**

### 4.3 Data privacy

- **You will hold behavioral data about gamblers.** Comprehensive state privacy statutes now number 16+ and rising, with CCPA/CPRA (California) the largest exposure. **Confidence: High.**
- **Is gambling behavior a "sensitive" category anywhere? No authority located.** No state comprehensive privacy law I found enumerates gambling behavior, betting activity, or problem-gambling status as sensitive data. **Confidence: Medium on the negative finding — do not treat as settled**, particularly given that some states include "consumer health data" and problem gambling is a recognized behavioral-health condition (Washington's My Health My Data Act's definition of consumer health data is broad; **I located no authority applying it to gambling behavior**). **Confidence: Low** on that edge case. `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Precise geolocation *is* sensitive data in most state regimes, including Maryland's MODPA** (effective **Oct. 1, 2025**). This matters directly: **your geo-blocking implementation collects the exact category that triggers heightened obligations.** Use coarse geolocation (state-level) where sufficient, minimize retention, and disclose the purpose. **Confidence: Medium-High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **MODPA is the strictest floor located:** blanket **prohibition on the sale of sensitive data** (first of its kind), strict data-minimization ("strictly necessary to provide the requested product or service"), heightened children's-privacy rules, and a 2026 amendment extending sensitive data to **inferred** sensitive data. **Confidence: Medium-High.** `[REQUIRES REVIEW BY LICENSED GAMING ATTORNEY]`
- **Practical posture:** do not sell or share personal data for cross-context behavioral advertising; publish a compliant privacy notice with CCPA rights; honor Global Privacy Control; keep a data map. Building to CCPA + MODPA covers most of the field. **Confidence: Medium.**

## 6. Kill-Criteria — what would make the honest recommendation "do not launch"

Any **one** of the following, if confirmed, is a stop:

1. **Payments unavailable.** Both Stripe and a mainstream alternative decline the business model in writing, and the only path is a high-risk acquirer at 2x–5x processing with a double-digit rolling reserve. At a low subscription price point this destroys unit economics. **Test this first — it is cheap, fast, and dispositive.**
2. **Counsel confirms that a dissemination-of-gambling-information statute (Oklahoma-type or Illinois-type) plausibly reaches a subscription picks service, in more than a handful of states**, such that the viable allowlist shrinks below roughly 20 states or excludes the largest legal-betting markets (NJ, PA, MI, OH, IL, NY).
3. **Counsel identifies an express tout/information-service licensing requirement reaching consumer-facing sellers in a target state** — particularly if Nevada's NRS 463.160 is read to reach out-of-state B2C operators, given the 1–20 year / $50,000 penalty. My research suggests it does not, but **this negative finding is the single most important thing for counsel to verify** because the downside is criminal, not civil.
4. **The performance record cannot be substantiated to the FTC standard.** If there is no immutable, timestamped, complete pick log covering the claimed period — if the "58% ATS" is a backtest, a reconstructed figure, or computed with a methodology chosen after seeing results — **the number cannot be published.** If the business does not work without publishing a headline win rate, and the win rate cannot be substantiated, the business does not work. This is the most likely real-world kill.
5. **Both Meta and Google Ads deny authorization and no viable organic/owned acquisition channel exists.** Not a legal failure but a commercial one that regulation causes. If CAC only works on paid social/search and both are closed, stop.
6. **The model's true edge is not durable.** Out of scope for this memo (another team member owns it) — but note the compliance interaction: **a marginal edge plus a marketing claim is a fraud exposure, not just a weak product.** The *Meyer* prosecution was not about bad picks; it was about claimed win rates that the record did not support. If the honest, substantiable, live win rate is not comfortably above break-even after vig (~52.4% ATS at -110), the compliant version of the marketing is unsellable, and the sellable version of the marketing is not compliant. **That is a kill criterion, and it is the one most likely to be rationalized away.**
7. **Founder unwilling to fund gaming counsel** at the $8,000–$25,000 initial-opinion level. Launching on this memo alone is not an acceptable risk posture; this memo is research, and several of its most important findings are negative findings from a research environment where primary statutory sources (Nevada Legislature, Justia, Cornell LII, eCFR, ftc.gov, gaming.nv.gov) were **blocked and could not be read directly**. See the limitation note below.

**Findings that would NOT be kill criteria** (manageable, priced into § 5): needing to geo-block 11–13 states; needing to drop affiliate revenue; needing to build California-ARL-grade subscription mechanics; needing 21+ gating; needing to run acquisition through content/email/podcast rather than paid social; App Store rejection (web-first distribution is viable).

1. Does NRS 463.160's information-service licensing requirement, read against the NRS 463.01642 definition, reach a consumer-facing (B2C) subscription picks service — and does Nevada assert jurisdiction over an out-of-state operator with Nevada subscribers? What is the current fee schedule, if it applies? (Criminal exposure: 1–20 years / $50,000.)
2. Does 720 ILCS 5/28-1(a)(9) reach a paid subscription that transmits point spreads, totals, and predictions to Illinois residents, and does any exemption in Article 28 or in the Illinois Sports Wagering Act cover an unlicensed information seller?
3. Does 21 O.S. § 987 reach the same conduct as to Oklahoma residents, and is there any Oklahoma enforcement history against information sellers?
4. **Complete the fifty-state (or allowlist-state) survey of dissemination-of-gambling-information and gambling-promotion statutes.** Which states beyond OK and IL have analogous provisions?
5. Confirm that the "engaged in the business of betting or wagering" element of 18 U.S.C. § 1084(a) excludes a flat-fee information seller, and identify the specific revenue structures (affiliate commissions, revenue share, contingent pricing) that would breach it.
6. Confirm that a **flat, non-outcome-contingent** subscription fee is not a "bet or wager" under 31 U.S.C. § 5362(1)(A) and not a wager under the gambling codes of each allowlist state — and specify which alternative pricing structures (money-back guarantees, performance-contingent fees, tiered refunds) would change that answer.
7. What substantiation package is sufficient for a published win-rate claim in this category, and what retention period applies? Should live and backtested results be separately presented, and what disclosure accompanies each?
8. Draft-review the specific launch claims. Which words are safe: "edge," "advantage," "model," "beat the closing line," "58% ATS over N games"? Which are not?
9. Do the sports-wagering advertising rules of the allowlist states (esp. Ohio Admin. Code 3775-16-08, 205 CMR 256.00) reach an unaffiliated, non-licensee subscription seller who links to no sportsbook?
10. Confirm the California ARL build requirements (consent capture, annual reminders, click-to-quit, 3-year record retention) and identify which additional states' auto-renewal statutes impose materially different obligations.
11. Is any privacy statute's "consumer health data" or "sensitive data" definition capable of capturing gambling-behavior or problem-gambling inferences? What are the obligations attaching to the geolocation data collected for geo-blocking?
12. If affiliate revenue is later added: full affiliate registration/licensing map, fees, and timelines for the allowlist states.
13. What is the current status and enforceability of the Utah/Texas/Louisiana app-store age-verification acts, and what developer-side obligations attach if an app is shipped?
14. Confirm PayPal's and Stripe's current written policy text as to handicapping/tips services, and obtain written merchant acceptability before build.
15. Does the pending CFTC/prediction-market circuit split change the analysis for marketing picks to residents of non-wagering states?
